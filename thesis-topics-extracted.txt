Sim-to-Real Transfer of Hip Assistance Policies for Multi-Terrain Walking\nThis project aims to train hip-assistance control policies in physics simulation and transfer them to a real wearable setup for multi-terrain walking (flat ground, stairs, and slopes). The scientific focus is the simulation-to-reality gap: domain randomization, privileged teacher-to-student distillation, and adaptation from simulated states to wearable inertial measurement unit signals and joint encoders available on hardware.\nIn simulation, the student will learn assistance profiles that improve engineering measures such as mechanical work proxies, torque smoothness, and walking stability. On the real side, policies will be deployed or fine-tuned using an existing hip-assistance platform or single-joint laboratory bench with off-the-shelf inertial sensors—incremental hardware use only, not a new exoskeleton design. Quantitative deliverables include sim-versus-real tracking error, success rate before and after transfer, calibration time on a new user, and ablations of randomization and adaptation methods. Computer vision is optional and secondary; inertial and proprioceptive sensing are the primary real-world interface.\n\n2. Egocentric Vision for Upcoming Terrain Prediction and Assistance Mode Switching\nThis project aims to predict upcoming walking terrain—flat ground, stairs, and slopes—from egocentric (first-person) video several meters ahead of the user, so that an assistive wearable can switch assistance modes in time. Lightweight image and video models will classify terrain, estimate stair or slope onset timing, and feed a software state machine for mode switching.\nDatasets will combine public egocentric walking video with optional short campus recordings under approved low-risk protocols. Deliverables emphasize quantitative tables: per-class precision, recall, and F1 scores, confusion matrices, onset timing error, and accuracy–latency trade-offs on ordinary computing hardware. This is the dedicated computer-vision topic in the set; no custom mechanical design is required.\n\n3. Gait Phase and Transition Intent Prediction from Wearable Inertial Sensors\nThis project aims to predict continuous gait phase, foot-contact events, and discrete movement transitions (such as sit-to-stand and walk-to-stair) from wearable inertial measurement units for assistance timing. Sequence models will be trained on public gait datasets and/or approved volunteer recordings, with optional comparison against public surface electromyography—without new hospital-grade muscle or brain recording.\nEvaluation will report phase error, event timing error in milliseconds, transition F1 scores, leave-one-subject-out generalization, sample-efficiency curves, and edge inference latency. Camera sensing is not required; the project stays signal- and learning-focused so it complements, rather than duplicates, the vision topic.\n\n4. Benchmarking Safe Assistance Controllers under Fault Injection with Optional Real-Joint Validation\nThis project aims to benchmark software controllers that keep wearable assistance safe when faults occur—command delay, noisy force estimates, joint-axis mismatch, or sudden trip-like disturbances. Controllers such as fixed impedance, variable impedance, and a simple predictive or barrier-style safety filter will share a common interface and be scored under a frozen fault-injection suite in simulation.\nAn optional incremental step is to replay the best controller on an existing laboratory joint. Quantitative results include peak torque and force reduction, time to soften after a spike, tracking error, and recovery success rate over many randomized trials. Medical soft-tissue endpoints are out of scope. The emphasis is control software and statistics, not imaging.\n\n5. Open Multimodal Dataset Curation, Labeling Tools, and Baseline Models for Wearable Locomotion\nThis project aims to build reusable data infrastructure for wearable locomotion research by curating synchronized inertial streams, joint or assistance logs, and—where useful—accompanying video for tasks such as terrain recognition, gait-phase estimation, and assistance-mode recommendation. The student will implement synchronization and quality-check tools, define standard train/validation/test splits, and release classical-feature and deep-learning baselines with one-command reproduction scripts.\nSuccess is measured by dataset clarity, legal reuse, leaderboard-style baseline tables with multi-seed variance, and whether another student can reproduce results from a clean checkout. Video may be included as one modality but is not the sole focus; inertial and kinematics data are first-class.\n\n6. Early Balance-Loss Prediction for Older Adult Users from Wearable Sensors\nThis project aims to predict near-term loss of balance for older adult users primarily from wearable inertial sensors (and optional pressure insoles if already available), with an early-warning horizon on the order of 0.5 to 2.0 seconds. Models will output a probability of near-term instability or a continuous stability score that an assistive system could consume as a software flag.\nPrefer public older-adult, balance, or perturbation datasets; any new volunteer protocol must follow low-risk ethics approval—not hospital fall-patient trials. A light optional vision arm may be added for comparison, but inertial prediction and operating-point analysis (lead time versus false alarms) are the core. Claims remain at engineering prediction performance, not clinical fall-prevention efficacy.\n
